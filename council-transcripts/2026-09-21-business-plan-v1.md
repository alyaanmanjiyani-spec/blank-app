# Council transcript — trial 1 (v1 skill)

- **Date:** 2026-09-21
- **Skill version:** upstream + 2 bug fixes (commit `f7b084f`)
- **Design:** 5 personas, one shared framing, one model, 5 reviewers, no dissent pass
- **Question:** Is this business start-up a good idea, or does it waste time and money?
  (16-year-old in Euless TX; productized analytics service for route-based local
  businesses; $250 Customer Profitability Report; goal is 6–8 case studies for
  fall-2028 college applications; zero execution to date.)

## Design note

All five advisors received an identical ~1,200-word framing written by the
orchestrator. This is the upstream design and is the primary correlation source
identified afterwards: one lens applied before any advisor reasoned.

## Advisor round

### The Contrarian

> Your plan contains a contradiction that kills it, and it isn't the parent approval or the FAFSA.
>
> **The offer cannot produce the deliverable.** You defined success as 6–8 case studies, each requiring "a change the owner ACTUALLY made" and "a measured result." Then you designed a product that explicitly excludes ongoing work: one report, a 20-minute call, done. Nothing in the $250 offer brings you back 60 days later to measure anything. You have no contractual reason to contact the client again, no access to the new data, and no leverage when they ghost you. So the modal outcome is 8 clients and zero case studies — eight "practices" by your own definition. You built a transaction and need a relationship.
>
> **Your own numbers already fail.** Cap of 2/month, May blacked out, no fall 2028 — that's ~20 slots a year at an average near $300. Roughly $6k. You benchmarked against $10,900 and concluded it "ties" a part-time job. It doesn't. It loses, at higher risk, with a 100% chance of rejection days. The money case was never real; stop pretending it was retired by choice.
>
> **The 10,000 words are the symptom, not preparation.** You flagged avoidance-as-productivity four times and then wrote 10,000 more words. Your kill conditions are self-graded by the person being graded. Nov 2026 will arrive and you will "diagnose."
>
> **The fix:** restructure to a $0 → $400 two-stage engagement. Stage one free: the report. Stage two, 8 weeks later, $400: the measurement. The free report is the only thing that gets financial records out of an owner for a 16-year-old anyway, and the paid half is exactly the artifact colleges see. Redesign that this week. Then make five calls Saturday.

### The First Principles Thinker

> You're asking "is this business a good idea?" The business is not the thing. Look at your own rankings: you want case studies, not money, and you ranked "owning something long-term" LAST. So you don't want a business. You want artifacts of analytical work on real data with a real decision-maker. A business is one route to that — and for a 16-year-old, it's the most expensive, slowest, most legally encumbered route available.
>
> Strip it down. What does a "complete case study" actually require? An owner with a question, data, a change made, a measured result. Notice what's absent: an invoice. Every hard part of your plan — the $250 price, FAFSA exposure, self-employment tax, Texas sales tax on IT services, parent approval for receiving payment, the missing refund policy, the walk-in cold pitch you dread — exists *only* because you attached a price. Delete the price and 80% of the plan's mass vanishes and the college value drops by zero. You already told us money is deprioritized. Then actually deprioritize it.
>
> Second, the demand question is not "will owners pay a teenager $250?" It's "will anyone hand me six months of records?" That's the binding constraint, and price makes it harder, not easier. Free plus a signed confidentiality promise is the lowest-friction version of the exact same experiment.
>
> Biggest reason this fails: the plan's architecture is optimized for not having the first conversation. Five scripted interviews, a six-point scoring rubric, a 100-business list, a decision rule — that's a machine for deferring contact while feeling productive. The rubric can't score interviews that never happen.
>
> Fix: one person, this week, free, warm. Your parents' pool guy, your scout troop's lawn contractor. Ask for records, not money. Everything else is downstream of that yes.

### The Expansionist

> You're building a $250 report. The actual asset is the dataset, and you're giving it away for free.
>
> Every engagement hands you six months of real invoice-level data from a route operator. Eight clients is 8 × ~500 customer-months of DFW service-business economics — drive time vs. margin, payment lag by ZIP, churn precursors. Nobody has this. Not Jobber, not ServiceTitan, not any academic. That is a proprietary benchmark, and a benchmark is the thing you sell forever: "your Keller customers run 40% below the DFW median." Report #1 is $250. Report #9 with a benchmark behind it is $1,500 and clients ask for it quarterly.
>
> Three moves you're leaving on the table:
>
> **Lead with the Leak Finder, free.** It's a small build, it finds literal missing money, and it converts. Run it on 20 businesses, not 2/month. Your 2-client cap is a ceiling you invented before you knew the demand curve.
>
> **Make the nudge test a real field experiment.** You already proved cold-emailing professors works — that's your rarest skill. A DFW psych or marketing faculty member gets a field study on payment behavior with n=400 invoices they cannot otherwise collect. Co-authored working paper by fall 2028 plus case studies beats either alone.
>
> **Your review study is a media asset, not an email attachment.** "What 5-star pool companies do that 3-star ones don't" belongs in a trade newsletter, a Facebook route-operator group, a local podcast. Publish it and inbound solves your selling aversion permanently.
>
> Biggest failure mode: you designed for 6–8 case studies, so 6–8 is your maximum outcome. Design for a dataset instead and case studies fall out as a byproduct.

### The Outsider

> Put yourself in the pickup truck. A guy who cleans pools in Euless gets an email from a kid he's never met asking how many hours a month someone there "spends moving numbers between your software and a spreadsheet." He doesn't know. Nobody knows that number. His wife does the books on Sunday nights and she'd say "a while." The email dies there — not because he's hostile, because there's no answer he can type.
>
> Then the real ask: six months of every invoice. That's his entire business naked — what he charges the neighbor versus the guy across town, who's stiffing him, how thin it actually is. He wouldn't send that to his brother-in-law. "Customer Profitability Report" and "productized analytics service" are words from a different planet; he thinks "am I losing money on the Dawsons or not?"
>
> Two things an ordinary person will flinch at. "Normally this is $300" when nobody has ever paid $300 is a lie, and these guys spend all day reading people who are working an angle. And pulling his first name out of his Google reviews, then opening with "I noticed you handle same-day service" — that doesn't read as research, it reads as being watched.
>
> Biggest reason this fails: the plan is built entirely on strangers, and he has written down that he doesn't want to talk to strangers. He'll find AP Chem instead. Forever.
>
> Fix: stop cold outreach completely. He knows people who own trucks — scout parents, church, neighbors, DECA advisors' contacts. Ask one of them, free, this week.

### The Executor

> The plan isn't wrong, it's unrunnable. Eight steps deep before a single dollar moves, and steps 1-3 and 6 are all things you can do alone at a desk. That's not a sequence, that's a queue of hiding places.
>
> Biggest reason this fails: **step 1 is a 10-minute conversation he's labeled a blocker for everything.** "Confirm parent approval + payment method" is dinner tonight. The fact that it's still open in September, after 10,000 words, tells you the whole story.
>
> The fix, literally this week:
> - **Tonight:** ask the parent. Use their Zelle. Done. Sales tax and FAFSA do not matter at $2k/year of revenue — that's a research project he invented to delay. Revisit at $5k.
> - **Saturday:** walk into 15 businesses. Skip the 60-website email hunt entirely.
> - **Cut the notebook to zero.** Eight tables and clasp for a business with no customers is the purest form of the pattern he already identified. One Google Sheet, five columns.
>
> Second thing that kills it: he's never delivered the report once. Do one free, this month, for a family friend. If he can't get 6 months of invoices out of someone who likes him, he'll never get it out of a stranger.
>
> And drop the rehearsal step. He cold-emailed professors and won. He can talk to a lawn guy.

## Peer review round (5 reviewers, anonymized A–E)

Mapping: A = First Principles, B = Executor, C = Expansionist, D = Contrarian, E = Outsider.

**Result: 5/5 unanimous on both questions.** Every reviewer named D strongest and C the biggest blind spot.

Consolidated reviewer points:

- **On D (strongest, 5/5):** the only response that tested the offer against the
  stated success criteria and found them incompatible. A one-shot report creates
  no contractual reason to return in 60 days, so "a change the owner made" and
  "a measured result" can never be captured. Modal outcome: paying clients, zero
  case studies.
- **On C (blindest, 5/5):** prescribes scale — 20 businesses, a proprietary
  benchmark, a co-authored paper — to someone at literal zero with a documented
  month-two quit pattern. Scope expansion as avoidance. Also flagged: pooling
  eight competing operators' invoice data into a sellable benchmark is a consent
  problem, and n=8 is not a benchmark.
- **What all five missed** (raised in review, not by any advisor):
  1. Nobody challenged the 6–8 case-study target itself. Common App allows ~150
     characters per activity and one 650-word essay; two deep cases likely beat
     eight thin ones, and the inflated target is what generated the unrunnable plan.
  2. **Seasonality.** Pool, lawn and pest revenue is seasonal, so any before/after
     comparison measures the season, not the intervention. Fix: split one client's
     customer list and compare within-client.
  3. **Backward date math.** A measured result needs 60–90 days post-change; with
     the May blackouts and the fall-2028 cutoff, the last viable engagement starts
     early 2028, making client #1 due immediately.
  4. Every kill condition is self-graded by the person being graded.
  5. The free-work fix (A, E) damages D's criterion — owners with nothing at stake
     implement nothing. Nobody named that tension.
  6. Nobody priced the downside of a 16-year-old mishandling real business financials.

---

## Chairman verdict (summary)

Recommendation: do it, but restructure to a two-stage engagement (free report →
paid measurement 60–90 days later), source the first client warm, target 2 case
studies rather than 6–8, design measurement as a within-client split, cut the
tracking notebook to one sheet, and defer the tax/FAFSA research.

First action: ask the parents tonight — both for permission and for warm leads.

Chairman partially overruled the unanimous dismissal of C, preserving the
professor-collaboration idea on the grounds that cold-emailing academics is the
founder's one *proven* outreach skill.

judged most worth keeping.
